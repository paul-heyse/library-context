Abstract
Relational Concept Analysis (RCA) and Graph-FCA (GCA) are two extensions of Formal Concept Analysis (FCA) introduced in order to allow concept
analysis on multi-relational data. The two methods have diﬀerent properties and parameters, but when restricting to binary relationships, existential
quantifier and unary concepts, their outputs look similar. On this basis, a
theoretical comparison of the two methods is conducted, showing that each
RCA concept corresponds to a GCA concept. Furthermore, to allow the comparison of concept intensions, a transformation of RCA results into relational
patterns is performed. These results give a sound basis to help interpreting
RCA results and to combine the two approaches for data exploration.
Keywords: Relational Concept Analysis, Graph-FCA, Relational Pattern,
Relational Data, Graph Pattern
1. Introduction
In many fields, collected data are not only tabular data but complex relational data that can be represented by graphs. For instance in the environmental domain, graphs are used to represent the social interactions between
animals, or in semantic web, graphs are used to model knowledge. An important task is then the extraction of structures, or patterns, from relational

Concept Analysis (FCA) [1] is a mathematical formalism based on lattice
theory and designed to analyze data (called a formal context) by grouping
objects with shared properties into formal concepts. It produces a hierarchical structure called a concept lattice representing the generalization ordering
between formal concepts. Designed for tabular data, the direct application
of FCA on more complex data is limited because it requires the flattening of
these data, which results in a loss of structural information.
Several generalizations of FCA have been made to handle multi-relational
data such as: Triadic Concept Analysis (TCA) [2], Power Context Families
[3], Relational Concept Analysis (RCA) [4], concept lattices of a relational
structure [5], Graph-FCA (GCA) [6] and Polyadic Relational Concept Analysis [7]. Other approaches deal with a single relationship, such as Network
Formal Contexts [8]. Among them two approaches seem to be very close:
RCA and GCA. RCA has been successfully used in a number of fields, e.g.
software engineering [9], ontology restructuring [10], the analysis of old Arabic remedies [11]. GCA has been used for diﬀerent purposes: extraction of
linguistic structures from parse trees, extraction of workflow patterns from
cooking recipes [12], or addressing the problem of knowledge graph alignment
[13]. RCA and GCA seem to be complementary and a few works have addressed questions of comparison or links between both approaches [14, 15, 16].
In [17], an empirical comparison of RCA and GCA has been conducted.
In this paper we propose a theoretical comparison of GCA and RCA based
on the same ground, i.e., using the existential quantifier, data with only unary
and binary relations, and unary concepts. We first prove that RCA concept
extents are included in GCA concept extents and secondly, that RCA concept
intents are included in GCA concept intents. However, as RCA and GCA
concept intents are defined diﬀerently, a common representation is required
for the comparison. We thus transform the RCA concept lattice family into
relational patterns that are similar to GCA graph patterns.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses the related work.
Section 3 presents RCA and GCA methods. The extensional comparison of
RCA and GCA is described in Sect. 5. Section 6 transforms the family of
RCA lattices into relational patterns and Sect. 7 compares the RCA patterns
to GCA graph patterns. Section 8 concludes and draws some perspectives.

2. Related work
RCA produces as output a family of concept lattices, which can be
diﬃcult to interpret when the lattices are large because a concept intent
can recursively depend on the intent of another relationally-related concept.
Various approaches have been proposed to deal with this complexity (e.g.
[18, 19]), some of them propose to transform the lattice family into a hierarchy of graphs, thus moving RCA close to GCA. The work in [14, 20] presents
a method to automatically extract directed acyclic graphs from RCA concept
intents, starting from one chosen lattice of the lattice family. The resulting
graphs are represented within a hierarchy in which each graph represents
a navigation path through the lattice family. This method was defined for
sequential data, described with a transitive relation. The aim was to explain
concept values in the target lattice from the preceding values, encoded in
other lattices. In [15], the authors propose a generic graphical representation
of RCA outputs that emphasizes the relational patterns. This representation
is named hierarchy of concept graphs where, each concept belongs to one
concept graph and each concept graph exhibits the relationships between
several concepts. This generic representation makes no assumption on the
context family and can therefore handle all kinds of relational data.
A practical comparison of TCA (Triadic Concept Analysis), RCA, and
GCA for modeling indeterminate values in ternary data has been realized in
[16]. Ternary relationships have been directly processed in GCA and TCA.
Indeed, GCA extends FCA to n-ary relationships and a ternary relationship
can be embedded into a TCA scheme i.e., “an object g has the attribute m
under the condition b". As RCA is limited to binary relations, the authors
propose and compare three diﬀerent encodings for the ternary relation. Similarities and dissimilarities between RCA and GCA have been studied in [17].
This conducted to the definition of a common ground, where the outputs of
the two methods are close to each other. Furthermore, this work explores,
and highlights how cycles are processed diﬀerently by both approaches. An
empirical comparison is carried out suggesting that RCA concept extents are
included in GCA concept extents. To our knowledge, a thorough theoretical
comparison of the two approaches has not yet been conducted.
3. Preliminaries
We here recall the fundamental notions of FCA, RCA and GCA.
3

3.1. Formal Concept Analysis (FCA)
FCA [1] consists of extracting conceptual structures from a set of objects
described by attributes, called a formal context. A formal context is a triple
K = (O, A, I) where, O and A are sets of objects and attributes respectively
and I is a binary relation between O and A, i.e., I ✓ O ⇥ A. For instance,
tables Animal and Food in Fig. 1 are examples of formal contexts. The
incidence relation I induces two mappings (operators), int : 2O ! 2A and
ext : 2A ! 2O such that int(X) = {y 2 A | 8x 2 X, (x, y) 2 I} and
ext(Y ) = {x 2 O | 8y 2 Y, (x, y) 2 I}. A formal concept is then defined
as a pair (X, Y ) where X ✓ O, Y ✓ A, int(X) = Y and ext(Y ) = X; X
and Y respectively represent the extent and the intent of the formal concept
(X, Y ). The set of all concepts built from a formal context K is denoted CK .
Let C1 = (X1 , Y1 ) and C2 = (X2 , Y2 ) two concepts of CK . The subsumption
relation v is defined by C1 v C2 if and only if X1 ✓ X2 (or equivalently
Y2 ✓ Y1 ). The set CK provided with the subsumption relation has a lattice
structure, and is called the concept lattice associated with K.
The FCA result on the Food context is the concept lattice presented in
Fig. 2 (right). Each concept is represented by a box that has three compartments representing, from top to bottom, the identifier, the intent and the
extent of the concept respectively. Dotted arrows represent the subsumption
relation between concepts.
3.2. Relational Concept Analysis (RCA)
RCA [4] extends the purpose of FCA to binary relational data: data represented by several tables and associations between them. In RCA, tables describing objects through attributes are called object-attribute contexts (formal contexts), and those encoding relations between objects are called objectobject contexts (relational contexts). RCA input data is called a Relational

are respectively the domain (source) and range (target) of rk . For instance,

3.3. Graph-FCA (GCA)
GCA [6] was introduced as an extension of FCA where the dataset is a
form of hyper-graph instead of a binary table. GCA adopts a graph point of
view, seeing objects as graph nodes, relationships as directed edges between
nodes, and attributes as node and edge labels. Whereas FCA defines its input
data as a formal context which is an incidence relationship between objects
and attributes, GCA defines its input data as a graph context which is an
incidence relationship between object tuples and attributes.

4. Overview
This work investigates a theoretical comparison of GCA and RCA based
on the same ground, i.e., using the existential quantifier (9), data with only
unary and binary relations, and unary concepts, leading to the result that
RCA concepts are included in GCA concepts. We first prove that all RCA
concept extents are GCA concept extents (extensional comparison) and secondly, that each RCA concept intent is included in some GCA concept intent
(intensional comparison).

9

The extensional comparison in Sect. 5 begins by defining a transformation
of relational context families into graph contexts, in order to provide a wellfounded ground for comparison (Definition 6). Definition 7 introduces how
a PGP simulates an RCA concept. This notion of PGP simulating an RCA
concept is used in Lemma 1 which states that if a PGP Q simulates an RCA
concept C, then ext(Q) = Ext(C). Lemma 1 is then used in Theorem 1 to
prove that for every RCA concept, there is a GCA concept with the same
extent. However, empirical results in a previous work [17] have shown that
RCA results get closer to GCA results by adding inverse relations to RCA
data as GCA automatically considers inverse relationships in its process.
Section 5.4 proves in Theorem 2 that adding inverse relations does not change
the previous result stating that all RCA extents are GCA extents.
To achieve the intensional comparison, a common representation is to be
found for RCA and GCA concept intents, since they are not directly comparable. Section 6 first presents a transformation of the RCA result (a concept
lattice family) into relational patterns including inverse relationships. The
translation process begins with the definition of the dependency graph of a
concept lattice family (Definition 8) and a concept pattern is then defined
as a Strongly Connected Component (SCC) of the dependency graph. Lemmas 3 and 4 respectively introduce the notions of redundant and strongly
redundant relational attributes, which form the basis for the definition of the
common representation (EAR-pattern, Definition 9) for comparing RCA and
GCA intensions. Definition 10 defines the RCA EAR-pattern which is equivalent in term of information to the original concept lattice family. Section 7
then addresses the intensional comparison proper, starting with the definition of GCA EAR-patterns (Definition 12), based on the quotient graphs of
GCA graph patterns (Definition 11). Theorem 3 finally states that the union
of RCA EAR-patterns is included in the union of GCA EAR-patterns in the
sense of labelled subgraphs (Definition 13).
5. Extensional Comparison of RCA and GCA
In this section and in the following, we compare RCA and GCA on the
same ground, as defined in [17]: considering data with only unary and binary
relations, using the existential quantifier (9) for RCA, computing only unary
concepts for GCA. We first compare RCA and GCA on the extensional side
because they are directly comparable. Indeed, the objects are the same on
both sides, although split in n subsets on the RCA side. As we only consider
10

unary GCA concepts, as mentioned above, this amounts to compare sets of
extensions, i.e., sets of sets of objects. Given CR the set of RCA concept
extensions, and CG the set of GCA concept extensions, we demonstrate that
CR ✓ CG , and that the reverse does not hold. Before stating and demonstrating this result, we need to make some observations regarding RCA concepts
w.r.t. their iterative generation process and also to translate an RCF to a
graph context in order to ground the comparison.
5.1. RCA Concept Rank and Initial Intent
The RCA concept generation process is iterative. On each iteration, new
concepts may be formed, and the intent of existing concepts may be extended
with new relational attributes derived from new concepts at the previous
iteration. A concept is identified by its extent, so a new concept is actually
a new extent. The iterative process stops when no new concept is formed,
which was proven to happen after a finite number of steps because the set of
objects is finite, hence the set of possible extents is also finite.
RCA usually only consider the concepts after the last iteration, i.e. pairs
(X, Y ) where Y is the final intent. We introduce two new notions: the rank
of a concept, and the initial intent of a concept.
Definition 4 (Concept rank). The rank of an RCA concept C = (X, Y ),
written rank (C), is the RCA iteration i
0 that first generates a concept
with extent X.
Definition 5 (Initial intent). The initial intent of an RCA concept C =
c
(X, Y ), written Int(C)
or shortly Yb , is the intent of C at iteration rank(C),
i.e., when extent X was first generated. We therefore have Yb 0 = X.

By definition of RCA iterations that can only add new relational attributes,
we have Yb ✓ Y . We can also say that the initial intent of a concept C
can only refer to concepts Ci (through relational attributes) s.t. rank (Ci ) <
rank (C) because a concept cannot be used before being introduced. Those
preliminary results are important because initial intents break the cycles that
may exist in the final intents of concepts. This hence establishes a topological
ordering among concepts via their rank, while preserving the characterization
of extents as X = Yb 0 .

6. From CLF to RCA Relational Patterns
In a concept lattice family, concept intents are defined in term of relational attributes pointing to other concepts while GCA concept intents are
defined in term of Projected Graph Pattern. Thus, concept intents of the
two approaches are not directly comparable, and a common representation
must be found. We choose graphs as the common representation because
graphs are natural settings for representing multi-relational data, and there
is work in this direction regarding RCA [14, 15]. In order to get as close
as possible to GCA, inverse relations will be included in the RCA data [17].
In the following, we detail the process of representing the RCA results into
relational patterns similar to GCA graph patterns.
17

6.1. Dependency Graph of a Concept Lattice Family
The transformation of a concept lattice family into a hierarchy of concept
graphs was described by [15], based on the Strongly Connected Components
(SCC) [21] of a dependency graph between concepts. The dependancy graph
included the relations from each concept in a lattice to its ancestors and to
the target concepts of its relational attributes, as defined below.
Definition 8 (Dependency graph [15]). Let L be a CLF. The dependency graph of L is the directed graph GL = (V, E) where: V is the set of
all concepts of all lattices in L, except bottom concepts, and E is the set of
concept dependencies C1 ! C2 , when C2 is a parent concept of C1 (C1 C2 ),
or C1 is labeled by a relational attribute 9 r(C2 ) i.e., 9 r(C2 ) 2 Int(C1 ).
For instance, in the CLF presented in Fig. 2, we have C_animals_2 !
C_animals_3 and C_animals_2 ! C_food_3. Figure 7 shows the dependency graph of the RCF presented in Fig. 6 with its 3 SCCs circled. For
sake of understanding, edges are represented in two ways: the dotted edges

labelled edges materialize the relational attributes between concepts. For instance, the edge between the concepts C_animals_2 and C_food_3 labelled
by 9 eat materializes the fact that 9 eat(C_food_3) 2 Int(C_animals_2).
A concept graph (or concept pattern) is defined as an SCC of the dependency graph i.e., a maximal set of concepts where each concept has a
dependency path to all other concepts (in the SCC) for the definition of its
intent. In these concept patterns, we will only keep the edges corresponding
to relational attributes (we know how to reconstruct the edges corresponding
to the subsumption relation). There are two diﬃculties in obtaining GCAlike patterns from these concept patterns. The first diﬃculty concerns the
inter-SCC edges (for instance, the edge 9 eat between concepts C_animals_1
and C_food_4 in Fig. 7), do we lose information on the concept intents by
ignoring them? The second diﬃculty is the presence of inverse relations that
are not explicitly represented in the GCA graph patterns. It is worth noting
that inter-SCC edges do not have inverse edges, otherwise they would be
intra-SCC. Both diﬃculties are solved in the next section.

• LV : V ! 2A is a labelling function from nodes to sets of attributes;

8. Conclusion
In this paper, we have conducted a theoretical comparison between RCA
and GCA based on their common ground namely, unary concepts, existential quantifier, data with only unary and binary relations and integration of
inverse relationships into RCA input data. The extensional comparison provides the theoretical proof that the set of RCA concept extents is included in
the set of GCA concept extents. To perform the intensional comparison, we
26

transform RCA output - the concept lattice family - into RCA EAR-patterns
where relational attributes are represented as labeled edges between concept
extensions. We then proved that the RCA EAR-patterns are subgraphs of
the GCA EAR-patterns, which are quotient graphs of GCA graph patterns.
These two comparisons allow us to conclude that the results of RCA are included in the results of GCA. Therefore, GCA is more expressive than RCA
in this setting. This is to explore further, considering a maximal version of
RCA, as suggested by [23].
As future work, we will investigate the formalization of RCA EARpatterns for lattice families with quantifiers other than the existential quantifier (98, ◆, or with percentages). This will lead to representations with
richer and more meaningful relations/edges, that will be useful to help the
interpretation of lattice families. The notion of n-ary concepts which is one
of the specificities of GCA is based on graph patterns. It would be interesting to compute n-ary concepts from RCA EAR-patterns to take advantage
of RCA quantifiers.
